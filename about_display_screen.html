WHY SOME SCREENS LOOK NATURAL AND COMFORTABLE
WHILE OTHERS LOOK HARSH, GLOWING, GRAINY, OR “ELECTRONIC”

A SIMPLE CAUSAL GUIDE TO PWM, FRC, PIXEL RESPONSE,
BRIGHTNESS, CONTRAST, SPECTRUM, SURFACE, AND THE “INK-LIKE” LOOK


============================================================
1. THE MAIN IDEA
============================================================

A monitor does not simply show a picture.

It produces light, changes that light over time, changes the
brightness of individual pixels, and sends that light through
a physical screen surface before it reaches your eyes.

Two monitors can display the same webpage and have the same
resolution, yet look completely different.

One may look:

- solid
- clean
- natural
- calm
- like ink on a surface

Another may look:

- bright
- glowing
- grainy
- sparkly
- hazy
- overly electronic
- visually intense

The important point is:

NO SINGLE SPECIFICATION EXPLAINS THIS.

“IPS”
“VA”
“8-bit”
“Flicker-free”
“Low blue light”
“1000:1 contrast”
“1 ms response time”

None of these alone tells you how the screen will actually
look to your eyes.

The useful question is:

WHAT IS THIS PARTICULAR DISPLAY ACTUALLY DOING?


============================================================
2. THE BIG PICTURE
============================================================

A simplified chain is:

DISPLAY HARDWARE
      ↓
LIGHT SOURCE / OLED EMISSION
      ↓
SPECTRUM + BRIGHTNESS
      ↓
BRIGHTNESS CONTROL
      ↓
PWM OR DC DIMMING
      ↓
COLOR PROCESSING
      ↓
FRC / TEMPORAL DITHERING
      ↓
PIXEL RESPONSE
      ↓
PIXEL / SUBPIXEL STRUCTURE
      ↓
GAMMA + TONAL TRANSITIONS
      ↓
SCREEN SURFACE
      ↓
REFLECTION + SCATTERING + SPARKLE
      ↓
FINAL IMAGE ENTERING THE EYE
      ↓
RETINAL SIGNAL
      ↓
VISUAL PROCESSING
      ↓
FOCUS + TRACKING + ATTENTION
      ↓
PERCEIVED IMAGE
      ↓
SOLID / CLEAN / NATURAL
OR
GLOWING / GRAINY / SPARKLY / HARSH


The final appearance is therefore the result of many layers
working together.


============================================================
3. SCREEN LIGHT IS DIFFERENT FROM PRINTED PAPER
============================================================

Printed paper mainly reflects light from the room.

A monitor produces its own light.

That difference matters.

A printed page can look like:

    white paper
    + black ink
    + room illumination

A monitor can look like:

    light source
    + pixel modulation
    + glowing white
    + glowing colors
    + dark pixels
    + surface scattering

This is one reason a good display can feel “ink-like” without
actually behaving like paper.

INK-LIKE DOES NOT MEAN THAT THE SCREEN IS LITERALLY EMULATING
PAPER.

It means the visual information appears:

- solid
- clean
- stable
- controlled
- naturally integrated with the surface

rather than appearing to float inside a glowing electronic
light source.


============================================================
4. PWM: BRIGHTNESS CAN CHANGE OVER TIME
============================================================

PWM means Pulse Width Modulation.

Instead of continuously reducing the light output, a display
can rapidly switch the light source between ON and OFF.

For example:

    ON  ON  ON  OFF  OFF
    ON  ON  ON  OFF  OFF
    ON  ON  ON  OFF  OFF

The eye may perceive this as one average brightness.

But physically, the light output is changing over time.

A different display may use DC dimming, where the light source
is reduced more continuously.

This gives two fundamentally different situations:

    PWM
    = brightness changes over time

    DC dimming
    = brightness is reduced more continuously

“Flicker-free” generally refers to reducing or avoiding
problematic brightness modulation.

It does NOT automatically mean:

    FRC-free
    perfectly stable pixels
    clean surface
    comfortable spectrum
    good contrast
    ink-like appearance


============================================================
5. FRC: COLOR CAN ALSO CHANGE OVER TIME
============================================================

FRC means Frame Rate Control.

It is used to simulate color levels that the panel cannot
produce directly.

Suppose a panel cannot directly produce the exact shade
required.

It may alternate between two nearby shades:

    Shade A
    Shade B
    Shade A
    Shade B

Your visual system can integrate this over time and perceive
something between them.

This is temporal dithering.

So:

PWM and FRC are NOT the same thing.

PWM:

    changes the light output itself.

FRC:

    changes pixel values to simulate another shade.

A display can therefore be:

    PWM-free but still use temporal dithering.

Or:

    temporally stable but use PWM.

Or:

    have both.

Or:

    have neither in a noticeable form.


============================================================
6. WHY FRC CAN BE HARD TO SEE
============================================================

Temporal dithering is often very subtle.

You may not see a pixel visibly flashing.

Instead, you might perceive:

- slight shimmer
- crawling texture
- unstable dark areas
- fine grain
- moving noise
- a faint vibrating quality

The effect can be particularly difficult to identify because
the image may look perfectly normal when viewed casually.

This is why a gradient test is useful.

A gradient can reveal whether apparently smooth colors are
actually being produced by stable pixel values or by rapid
alternation between nearby values.

But:

SMOOTH GRADIENT ≠ TEMPORALLY STABLE GRADIENT.

A display can have a beautiful smooth gradient while using
temporal dithering.

Another display may show slight banding while remaining
temporally more stable.

Therefore:

BANDING AND TEMPORAL STABILITY ARE DIFFERENT PROBLEMS.


============================================================
7. NATIVE 8-BIT IS NOT THE SAME AS “NO DITHERING”
============================================================

8-bit per channel provides:

    256 levels

for each red, green, and blue channel.

8-bit + FRC can use temporal alternation to simulate additional
levels.

But the important distinction is:

    NATIVE COLOR DEPTH
    and
    TEMPORAL BEHAVIOR

are not exactly the same question.

A display can have a high-quality color specification while
still performing temporal processing somewhere in the display
pipeline.

Therefore:

“8-bit” is useful information.

It is NOT proof that the image is temporally static.


============================================================
8. DITHERING CAN HAPPEN IN DIFFERENT PLACES
============================================================

Temporal or spatial dithering can potentially be introduced at
different stages:

    application
        ↓
    operating system
        ↓
    GPU
        ↓
    display interface
        ↓
    monitor electronics
        ↓
    panel

But you should NOT assume that every device dithers at every
stage.

The exact implementation depends on the hardware and software.

This is why a registry setting or GPU option is not automatically
proof that a monitor is using FRC.

It may be a diagnostic clue.

It is not the same as directly measuring the display.


============================================================
9. PIXEL RESPONSE TIME IS A DIFFERENT VARIABLE
============================================================

Pixel response time describes how quickly a pixel changes from
one state to another.

Slow response can produce:

- ghosting
- trailing
- motion blur
- dark smearing

Fast response can produce:

- clearer motion
- sharper transitions

But:

FAST ≠ AUTOMATICALLY BETTER FOR YOUR EYES

and:

SLOW ≠ AUTOMATICALLY MORE COMFORTABLE

Pixel speed and temporal dithering are different mechanisms.

A slow pixel does not automatically eliminate FRC.

A fast pixel does not automatically create FRC.

They simply affect how quickly the physical pixel can follow
changes in its requested state.


============================================================
10. VERY SLOW PIXELS CAN CHANGE HOW MOTION LOOKS
============================================================

Imagine moving black text across a white background.

With a slow panel:

    BLACK → gray trail → white

The moving object leaves a visible trail.

With a faster panel:

    BLACK → WHITE

The transition is cleaner.

This matters because the eye receives a different moving image.

For general display quality, the target is not:

    “as slow as possible”

or:

    “as fast as possible.”

The target is:

    CLEAN, CONTROLLED TRANSITIONS.


============================================================
11. BRIGHTNESS IS NOT JUST A COMFORT SETTING
============================================================

Brightness determines how much light reaches your eyes.

If white areas are extremely bright, the screen can feel
strongly emissive.

For example:

    black text on moderate white
    → controlled

versus:

    black text on extremely bright white
    → much stronger visual stimulus

This is one reason a screen can be technically flicker-free
yet still feel harsh.

Brightness also affects the perceived character of the image.

Too much brightness can make the screen feel:

    glowing
    shiny
    electronic

Moderate brightness can make the same image appear more:

    solid
    controlled
    natural


============================================================
12. CONTRAST IS NOT SIMPLY “HIGH = BAD”
============================================================

Contrast describes the difference between dark and bright
parts of the image.

High contrast can make text extremely clear.

For example:

    BLACK TEXT
    on
    WHITE BACKGROUND

can be very readable.

But very strong luminance differences can also make an image
feel visually intense.

This does NOT mean low contrast is automatically better.

The useful target is:

    CLEAR CONTRAST
    without
    EXCESSIVE LUMINANCE INTENSITY.


============================================================
13. LOCAL CONTRAST AND EDGES
============================================================

The visual system is particularly sensitive to edges.

An edge is where brightness changes quickly:

    BLACK | WHITE

rather than:

    GRAY | SLIGHTLY DARKER GRAY

This is part of normal visual processing.

The retina and later visual systems emphasize boundaries because
edges contain important information about objects.

This is useful for reading.

But extremely strong local contrast can also make a display feel
more intense.

This is related to phenomena such as Mach bands, where the visual
system can perceive brightness differences near boundaries more
strongly than the physical luminance difference alone would
suggest.

This does NOT mean:

    “high contrast damages the eyes.”

It means that contrast is part of the visual signal being
processed.


============================================================
14. GAMMA CONTROLS HOW TONES ARE DISTRIBUTED
============================================================

Gamma affects how digital brightness values are converted into
actual displayed luminance.

Two displays can have the same:

    black level
    white level
    contrast ratio

yet distribute intermediate tones differently.

This changes how the image looks.

If dark tones are lifted too much, blacks can look:

    gray
    cloudy
    hazy

instead of solid.

The image can develop a kind of:

    WHITE VEIL

over the dark areas.

This is the opposite of the visual quality often described as
“ink-like.”

A useful simplified description is:

    GOOD TONAL CONTROL
    = objects look solid

    POOR / WASHED TONAL CONTROL
    = objects look veiled


============================================================
15. WHAT “INK-LIKE” ACTUALLY MEANS
============================================================

“INK-LIKE” is NOT a technical specification.

You will not find a standard monitor measurement called:

    Ink-Like = 87%

It is a description of the final visual appearance.

An ink-like image tends to look:

    solid
    clean
    quiet
    stable
    naturally integrated with the screen surface

Text looks like a solid shape.

It does NOT appear to:

    glow
    sparkle
    crawl
    shimmer
    bloom
    float inside a haze

This is why “ink-like” is important.

It connects all the technical variables to the thing you
actually see.


============================================================
16. SHARP IS NOT THE OPPOSITE OF INK-LIKE
============================================================

This distinction is important.

A screen can be:

    SHARP + INK-LIKE

That is desirable.

The problem is not sharpness itself.

For example:

    sharp text
    + stable pixels
    + clean edges
    + moderate luminance
    + clean surface

can look extremely solid.

By contrast:

    sharp text
    + excessive brightness
    + sparkle
    + unstable pixels
    + strong glow

can look harsh.

Therefore:

    SHARP ≠ HARSH

and:

    SOFT ≠ COMFORTABLE

The desired quality is:

    SHARP
    +
    SOLID
    +
    STABLE
    +
    CONTROLLED


============================================================
17. THE SCREEN SURFACE MATTERS
============================================================

The LCD/OLED panel is not the final thing your eyes see.

There is also a physical surface in front of it.

Anti-glare coatings scatter incoming light.

This can reduce reflections.

But aggressive matte coatings can also produce visible
surface texture.

This may appear as:

    grain
    sparkle
    crystalline texture
    haze

The image itself may be perfectly stable, but the surface can
make it look less clean.

This is sometimes called:

    AG SPARKLE

where AG means anti-glare.

A heavily textured surface can therefore make text feel less
like clean ink.

A smoother surface can preserve a cleaner image.

But smoother surfaces can produce stronger reflections.

Therefore:

    MATTE
    = better reflection control
    but potentially more surface texture

    GLOSSY / SEMI-GLOSS
    = cleaner image
    but potentially more reflections

Neither is universally superior.


============================================================
18. WHY SURFACE TEXTURE CAN MATTER TO THE “INK-LIKE” LOOK
============================================================

Imagine the same black letter:

    █

On a clean surface, its edge can appear:

    solid and uniform

On a heavily textured surface, the same letter may appear:

    solid
    + tiny variations
    + grain
    + sparkle

The letter is still technically sharp.

But its visual surface is no longer perfectly clean.

That changes the perceived character.

So:

    PIXEL QUALITY
    +
    SURFACE QUALITY

both contribute to whether the image looks clean.


============================================================
19. SPECTRUM: THE LIGHT IS NOT ALL THE SAME
============================================================

A display's light has a spectral distribution.

Different backlights and OLED implementations produce different
mixtures of wavelengths.

For example, many LCD monitors use white LEDs whose spectrum
differs substantially from older CCFL displays.

This changes the character of the light.

Shorter wavelengths also scatter more strongly in the eye than
longer wavelengths.

However, this should not be turned into the simplistic claim:

    “blue light automatically causes eye damage”

or:

    “blue light automatically causes focusing spasms.”

Those are much stronger claims than the basic optical facts
support.

Instead, spectrum should be treated as another variable that can
affect the visual character of a display and can be tested
independently.


============================================================
20. COLOR TEMPERATURE IS NOT THE WHOLE SPECTRUM
============================================================

A monitor may allow:

    6500K
    7500K
    9300K

etc.

These settings change the balance of the displayed colors.

But a color-temperature number does not completely describe the
physical spectrum.

Two monitors can both be set to:

    6500K

while having different spectral distributions.

Therefore:

    COLOR TEMPERATURE ≠ COMPLETE SPECTRAL DESCRIPTION.


============================================================
21. IPS, VA, AND TN
============================================================

Panel type changes the physical behavior of the display.

IPS commonly provides:

    good viewing angles
    relatively consistent color across the screen

VA commonly provides:

    deeper blacks
    higher native contrast

but can have slower dark transitions.

TN commonly provides:

    fast transitions
    different viewing-angle behavior
    different contrast/color characteristics

These are general tendencies, not absolute rules.

There is no scientifically valid rule saying:

    IPS = bad for eyes
    VA = good for eyes
    TN = good for eyes

The actual implementation matters more than the label.


============================================================
22. BLACK LEVEL IS IMPORTANT FOR THE VISUAL CHARACTER
============================================================

Consider two displays.

Display A:

    black → truly dark

Display B:

    black → dark gray

Display B can make the entire image feel slightly washed.

Now add:

    bright white
    + raised blacks
    + moderate contrast

and the image may develop a cloudy appearance.

A display with good black control can make dark objects appear
more solid.

This contributes to the “ink-like” character.


============================================================
23. WHITE LEVEL MATTERS TOO
============================================================

The opposite problem is excessive white luminance.

If white is extremely bright:

    white areas can appear to emit strongly.

If white is controlled:

    the same page can appear more like a stable surface.

This is why a screen can have excellent black levels and still
feel visually aggressive.

Black level and white level must be considered together.


============================================================
24. “INK-LIKE” IS THE RESULT OF MULTIPLE THINGS WORKING TOGETHER
============================================================

A useful simplified model is:

    CONTROLLED BRIGHTNESS
            +
    CONTROLLED CONTRAST
            +
    CLEAN TONAL TRANSITIONS
            +
    LOW VISIBLE SURFACE SPARKLE
            +
    STABLE PIXEL OUTPUT
            +
    CLEAN PIXEL / SUBPIXEL STRUCTURE
            +
    SUITABLE SPECTRUM
            +
    ADEQUATE PIXEL RESPONSE
            ↓
    CLEAN, SOLID IMAGE
            ↓
    “INK-LIKE” APPEARANCE


No single component guarantees it.


============================================================
25. TEMPORAL CLEANLINESS + SPATIAL CLEANLINESS
============================================================

This is a useful way to organize the entire problem.

TEMPORAL CLEANLINESS

means:

    the image does not contain unwanted changes over time.

Possible sources:

    PWM
    FRC
    temporal dithering
    unstable pixel transitions

SPATIAL CLEANLINESS

means:

    the image looks physically clean across the screen.

Possible influences:

    surface texture
    AG sparkle
    pixel structure
    subpixel arrangement
    edge rendering
    gamma
    contrast
    haze

Then there is:

OPTICAL INTENSITY

influenced by:

    brightness
    white luminance
    contrast
    spectrum

The perceived image is the combination:

    TEMPORAL CLEANLINESS
            +
    SPATIAL CLEANLINESS
            +
    OPTICAL INTENSITY
            ↓
    FINAL VISUAL CHARACTER


============================================================
26. WHY “FLICKER-FREE” IS NOT ENOUGH
============================================================

Suppose a monitor has:

    no detectable PWM

but:

    aggressive AG sparkle
    very bright white
    washed blacks
    strong local contrast

It may still look unpleasant.

Another monitor may have:

    stable brightness
    clean surface
    controlled luminance
    good tonal separation
    stable pixel output

and look much more natural.

Therefore:

    FLICKER-FREE
    ≠
    VISUALLY CLEAN

and:

    FLICKER-FREE
    ≠
    INK-LIKE


============================================================
27. WHY “LOW BLUE LIGHT” IS NOT ENOUGH
============================================================

Reducing short-wavelength output changes the spectrum.

But a monitor can have low-blue-light characteristics and still
have:

    PWM
    FRC
    excessive brightness
    AG sparkle
    poor gamma
    washed blacks
    harsh contrast

Therefore:

    LOW BLUE LIGHT
    ≠
    COMFORTABLE DISPLAY

It is simply one variable.


============================================================
28. WHY “NATIVE 8-BIT” IS NOT ENOUGH
============================================================

Native 8-bit can reduce the need for certain forms of FRC.

But it does not tell you:

    brightness behavior
    PWM behavior
    surface quality
    spectrum
    gamma
    black level
    white luminance
    contrast behavior
    pixel response

Therefore:

    NATIVE 8-BIT
    ≠
    INK-LIKE


============================================================
29. WHY AN OLD SCREEN CAN SOMETIMES LOOK DIFFERENT
============================================================

Older LCDs may use:

    CCFL backlights
    different panel characteristics
    different coatings
    different gamma behavior
    lower peak luminance
    slower pixel response

A modern monitor can have much better specifications and still
look completely different.

This does not mean:

    OLD = GOOD
    NEW = BAD

It means that different generations can have different
combinations of:

    spectrum
    brightness
    coating
    contrast
    gamma
    pixel behavior

The visual character is determined by the combination.


============================================================
30. WHY “MORE ADVANCED” DOES NOT NECESSARILY MEAN “NICER”
============================================================

Technology is usually optimized for measurable performance:

    higher refresh rate
    faster response
    wider color gamut
    higher brightness
    HDR
    higher contrast
    thinner design

But a person's preferred visual appearance may be different.

For example:

    500-nit white

is technically more capable than:

    250-nit white.

But that does not mean the 500-nit display will look better
for every person or every task.

Similarly:

    1 ms response

does not automatically look better than:

    8 ms response.

The engineering target and the perceptual target are not always
the same.


============================================================
31. THE MOST IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:
TECHNICAL QUALITY VS PERCEIVED QUALITY
============================================================

TECHNICAL MEASUREMENTS tell you:

    what the display does.

YOUR EYES tell you:

    what that behavior looks like in practice.

Both matter.

For example:

    measured contrast
    measured gamma
    measured PWM frequency
    measured response time

are useful.

But if your actual question is:

    “Does this screen look clean and solid?”

you eventually have to look at the screen.

The correct approach is not:

    “Ignore measurements.”

Nor:

    “Trust measurements completely.”

Instead:

    MEASURE → UNDERSTAND → LOOK → COMPARE.


============================================================
32. A GOOD TESTING STRATEGY
============================================================

Change ONE variable at a time.

For example:

TEST 1
    brightness

TEST 2
    contrast

TEST 3
    color temperature

TEST 4
    surface / viewing environment

TEST 5
    temporal stability

TEST 6
    motion

TEST 7
    static text

TEST 8
    dark gradients

TEST 9
    bright white backgrounds

Do not change five settings simultaneously.

Otherwise you cannot tell which variable caused the difference.


============================================================
33. TEST PWM SEPARATELY
============================================================

A PWM test asks:

    “Does the light source change over time?”

It does NOT answer:

    “Does the panel use FRC?”

Therefore PWM measurement should be treated as one test.

If PWM is absent:

    continue testing.

Do not conclude:

    “The display is temporally perfect.”


============================================================
34. TEST FRC SEPARATELY
============================================================

Use carefully designed gradients and temporal tests.

Look for:

    shimmer
    crawling
    unstable texture
    fine grain that changes
    differences between supposedly static colors

Compare:

    static image
    normal display
    altered GPU settings

But remember:

A visual artifact does not automatically identify the exact
hardware mechanism.

The test can tell you:

    “Something changed temporally.”

Further measurement may be required to determine exactly why.


============================================================
35. LOOK AT DARK COLORS
============================================================

Dark areas can be particularly useful when investigating
temporal instability.

Use:

    dark gray
    near-black gradients
    dark neutral colors

and observe them closely.

The question is not:

    “Does the gradient look perfectly smooth?”

The question is:

    “Does the apparently static area remain visually stable?”


============================================================
36. LOOK AT TEXT
============================================================

Text is an excellent practical test because it combines:

    pixel structure
    edge contrast
    gamma
    brightness
    surface texture
    temporal stability
    subpixel rendering

Look at:

    black text on white
    gray text on white
    white text on dark gray
    small text
    medium text

Ask:

    Does the letter look solid?

or:

    Does the edge glow, sparkle, crawl, or appear fuzzy?


============================================================
37. LOOK AT WHITE
============================================================

Open a mostly white page.

Do not only ask:

    “Is it bright enough?”

Ask:

    “Does the white look like a clean surface,
     or like a light source?”

This is particularly important for the ink-like quality.

A good display does not necessarily need extremely dim white.

It needs CONTROLLED white.


============================================================
38. LOOK AT BLACK
============================================================

Open a dark image.

Ask:

    Are the dark areas genuinely dark?

or:

    Do they look gray, cloudy, or veiled?

Then compare the transition:

    black
      →
    dark gray
      →
    medium gray

Good tonal control can make these transitions appear solid
rather than hazy.


============================================================
39. LOOK AT A LARGE COLOR AREA
============================================================

Use a large area of:

    red
    green
    blue
    gray
    skin-like colors
    dark colors

Do not only judge color accuracy.

Look for:

    stability
    grain
    shimmer
    brightness
    surface texture

A technically accurate color can still look unpleasant if its
presentation is unstable or overly intense.


============================================================
40. THE “INK-LIKE” PRACTICAL TEST
============================================================

Instead of asking:

    “Is this monitor good?”

ask these questions:

    Do letters look SOLID?

    Do dark areas look SOLID?

    Does white look CONTROLLED rather than glowing?

    Do edges look CLEAN?

    Does the image remain STABLE?

    Is there visible GRAIN or SPARKLE?

    Do colors look NATURAL rather than electrically intense?

    Do black and gray tones remain distinct without a white veil?

    Does the image look clean without needing excessive
    brightness or contrast?


If the answer is consistently yes, the display may have the
visual character commonly described as:

    INK-LIKE.


============================================================
41. WHAT “INK-LIKE” DOES NOT MEAN
============================================================

It does NOT necessarily mean:

    low brightness
    low contrast
    old technology
    TN
    CCFL
    slow pixels
    matte coating
    glossy coating
    warm color temperature
    low blue light
    low refresh rate

These may influence the appearance.

None defines it.


============================================================
42. THE REAL TARGET
============================================================

The target is not:

    “find the oldest panel.”

It is not:

    “find IPS.”

It is not:

    “find VA.”

It is not:

    “find 8-bit.”

It is not:

    “find the highest PWM frequency.”

It is not:

    “find the lowest brightness.”

The target is:

    STABLE LIGHT OUTPUT
        +
    CONTROLLED BRIGHTNESS
        +
    CLEAN TONAL TRANSITIONS
        +
    APPROPRIATE CONTRAST
        +
    LOW UNWANTED TEMPORAL MODULATION
        +
    ADEQUATE PIXEL RESPONSE
        +
    CLEAN SURFACE
        +
    LOW DISTRACTING SPARKLE
        +
    SUITABLE SPECTRUM
        +
    CLEAN PIXEL / SUBPIXEL RENDERING
        ↓
    SOLID, CLEAN, NATURAL IMAGE
        ↓
    INK-LIKE VISUAL CHARACTER


============================================================
43. THE COMPLETE MODEL
============================================================

A display can be understood as four major layers.

LAYER 1 — LIGHT

    backlight / OLED emission
    spectrum
    brightness
    white luminance


LAYER 2 — TIME

    PWM
    DC dimming
    FRC
    temporal dithering
    pixel response


LAYER 3 — SPACE

    pixel structure
    subpixels
    gamma
    contrast
    black level
    surface coating
    AG sparkle
    reflections
    scattering


LAYER 4 — PERCEPTION

    edges
    motion
    focus
    tracking
    attention
    image stability
    perceived intensity

All four eventually combine into one thing:

    WHAT THE IMAGE LOOKS LIKE.


============================================================
44. THE MOST USEFUL MENTAL MODEL
============================================================

Do not think:

    “IPS is the problem.”

Do not think:

    “FRC is always the problem.”

Do not think:

    “PWM is always the problem.”

Do not think:

    “High contrast is always the problem.”

Do not think:

    “Blue light is the problem.”

Instead think:

    WHAT IS THE SCREEN ACTUALLY PRODUCING?


Then separate the possibilities:

    Is the light changing over time?
        → PWM

    Are pixel values changing to simulate colors?
        → FRC / temporal dithering

    Are pixels transitioning too slowly?
        → response behavior

    Is the white excessively bright?
        → luminance

    Are dark tones washed out?
        → black level / gamma / contrast

    Is the surface producing visible texture?
        → AG sparkle / scattering

    Is the spectrum different?
        → backlight / OLED spectral behavior

    Are edges excessively intense?
        → local contrast / tonal behavior

    Does the image look solid and stable?
        → overall perceptual result

    Does it look like clean information sitting naturally
    on a surface?

        → INK-LIKE

45. FINAL CAUSAL CHAIN
============================================================

DISPLAY DESIGN
      ↓

============================================================
46. THE FINAL RULE
============================================================

Do not judge a display from one specification.

Do not judge it from one technology label.

Do not judge it from “flicker-free.”

Do not judge it from “8-bit.”

Do not judge it from “1 ms.”

Do not judge it from “IPS.”

Do not judge it from “low blue light.”

Instead:

    IDENTIFY THE PHYSICAL BEHAVIOR
            ↓
    TEST EACH VARIABLE SEPARATELY
            ↓
    SEE HOW THE IMAGE ACTUALLY LOOKS
            ↓
    CHECK FOR TEMPORAL STABILITY
            ↓
    CHECK FOR SPATIAL CLEANLINESS
            ↓
    CHECK BRIGHTNESS / CONTRAST / TONES
            ↓
    CHECK THE SURFACE
            ↓
    JUDGE THE FINAL IMAGE

The ultimate practical question is:

    DOES THE IMAGE LOOK STABLE, SOLID, CLEAN,
    CONTROLLED, AND NATURAL?

If it does, the display may have the quality people often
describe as:

    “INK-LIKE.”

That perceptual result is more useful than any single
specification because it describes what all the individual
technical factors ultimately produce together.
