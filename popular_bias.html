Confirmation bias: This is when you only notice or look for information that agrees with what you already believe, and you ignore stuff that disagrees. Like if you think your favorite sports team is the best, you’ll remember every win and forget the losses.

Sunk-cost fallacy: This is when you keep doing something just because you’ve already put a lot of time, money, or effort into it, even if it’s clearly not working anymore. Example: staying in a bad relationship or finishing a terrible movie just because you already watched half of it.

Hindsight bias: This is the “I knew it all along” feeling. After something happens, you convince yourself you predicted it even though you didn’t. Like saying “I totally knew that team would lose” after the game is over.

Baader-Meinhof effect: Also called the frequency illusion. Once you notice something new (like a weird word or a car model), you suddenly start seeing it everywhere. It feels like it’s popping up more, but your brain is just paying attention to it now.

Similarity bias: This is preferring people who are like you — same interests, background, style, etc. You trust them more or think they’re cooler just because they remind you of yourself or your friends.

Conformity bias: This is going along with what the group thinks or does, even if you privately disagree, just so you fit in. Like laughing at a joke everyone else laughs at even if you don’t find it funny.

Dunning-Kruger effect: This is when people who know very little about something think they’re experts, while actual experts often doubt themselves more. Classic example: someone who watched two YouTube videos on a topic suddenly acts like they know everything.

Primacy bias: This is giving extra weight to the first information you get. First impressions stick hard. If the first thing you hear about a person is bad, it’s tough to change your mind later.

Negativity bias: Your brain pays more attention to bad stuff than good stuff. One mean comment can ruin your day even if you got ten nice ones. Evolution made us extra alert to threats.

Survivorship bias: Focusing only on the people or things that “made it” and ignoring all the ones that failed. Like looking at successful dropouts and thinking school doesn’t matter, while forgetting the huge number of dropouts who didn’t become rich.

Optimism bias: Thinking good things are more likely to happen to you than to other people, and bad things are less likely. “I’ll be fine driving without a seatbelt — accidents happen to other people.”

Availability bias: Judging how common or important something is based on how easily examples come to mind. If you just saw a plane crash on the news, you might feel flying is super dangerous even though it’s statistically very safe.

Anchoring bias: Relying too heavily on the first number or piece of info you hear (the “anchor”) when making decisions. If a jacket is first shown as $200 and then marked down to $100, it feels like a bargain even if it’s still overpriced.

Endowment effect: Once you own something, you suddenly value it more than before you owned it. You might refuse to sell your old hoodie for a fair price just because it’s “yours.”

Hostile attribution bias: Assuming other people’s actions are meant to be mean or against you, even when they’re neutral or accidental. Someone bumps into you in the hallway and you immediately think they did it on purpose.
